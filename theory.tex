\section{Biot-Savart field of a circular loop}

Omitting the dimensional constant $\mu_0/(4\pi$), the Biot-Savart
field of a closed current loop $\bfL(t)$ equals
\begin{equation}
\bfB(\bfx)=\oint\bfb(\bfx,t)\,dt,\quad
\bfb(\bfx,t) = \frac{\dot\bfL\times(\bfx-\bfL)}{D^3},\quad D\equiv|\bfx-\bfL|.
\end{equation}
A circular loop in the $(x,y)$ plane is defined parametrically by
$\bfL(t)=(r\cos(t),r\sin(t),0)$.\footnote{If the loop center is at
$(x, y, z)=(\xi, 0, \zeta)$ instead of the origin, the field formulas
remain the same under the change
$\bfB(x,y,x)\to\bfB(x-\xi,y,z-\zeta)$.}

Component-wise, we have
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
b_x &= \frac{\dot L_y(z-L_z) - \dot L_z(x-L_x)}{D^3} = \frac{\dot L_yz}{D^3},\\
b_y &= \frac{\dot L_z(x-L_x) - \dot L_x(z-L_z)}{D^3} = -\frac{\dot L_xz}{D^3},\\
b_z &= \frac{\dot L_x(y-L_y) - \dot L_y(x-L_x)}{D^3}
= \frac{L_x\dot L_y-L_y\dot L_x + \dot L_xy - \dot L_y x}{D^3}
= \frac{r^2 + \dot L_xy - \dot L_y x}{D^3}.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
Here the condition $L_z\equiv0$ is applied for a flat $(x,y)$ loop.  In terms of the parameter $t$,
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
b_x &= \frac{rz\cos(t)}{D^3},\\
b_y &= \frac{rz\sin(t)}{D^3},\\
b_z &= r\frac{r - x\cos(t) - y\sin(t)}{D^3},\\
D   &= \sqrt{(x - r\cos(t))^2 + (y - r\sin(t))^2 + z^2}
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
Note that, for $y=0$, $D$ is an even function of $t$, and $B_y=\int_{-\pi}^\pi b_y\,dt=0$.

Derivatives of the infinitesimal field:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
b_{x,x} &= -3rz\frac{\cos(t)(x-r\cos(t))}{D^5},\\
b_{y,y} &= -3rz\frac{\sin(t)(y-r\sin(t))}{D^5},\\
b_{z,z} &=  3rz\frac{x\cos(t) + y\sin(t) - r}{D^5},\\
b_{x,z} &=  \frac{r\cos(t)}{D^3}\left(1-3\frac{z^2}{D^2}\right),\\
b_{z,x} &= -\frac{r\cos(t)}{D^3} -3r\frac{(x-r\cos(t))(r-x\cos(t)-y\sin(t))}{D^5}.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
It is immediately verified that
$\bfna\cdot\bfb=b_{x,x}+b_{y,y}+b_{z,z}=0$, and therefore the total
field $\bfB$ is divergence free, as it should be.  Not so for the
infinitesimal $b_{x,z}$ and $b_{z,x}$.  The current-free condition
$B_{x,z}=B_{z,x}$ is valid only for the total field after $\oint dt$
over $[0,2\pi]$.  Proving this in general by integrating the
derivatives above by $t$ is very difficult, as elliptic integrals are
involved.  {\sl Mathematica} consistenly evaluates $B_{x,z}-B_{x,z}$ at
various random values of $(r,x,y,z)$ as exact zero, which cannot be a
coincidence.

\section{The null ("$X$") point}

The field in the plane $y=0$ is expressed through the vector potential
$A$ as $(B_x, B_z) = (-\partial_zA_y, \partial_xA_y)$, meaning that
the countour lines of $A_y(x, z)$ are the magnetic lines, and the $X$
point is a saddle point of $A_y$.  The $X$ point condition, however,
can be written in terms of $(B_x,B_z)$ only.  The field is zero at the
target point $(X,Z)$ and its Taylor expansion to first order is
\begin{equation}
  \begin{aligned}
B_x &= B_{x,x}(x-X)+B_{x,z}(z-Z),\\
B_z &= B_{z,x}(x-X)+B_{z,z}(z-Z).
  \end{aligned}
  \label{linear}
\end{equation}
The commas denote differentiation.  Unlike the field $\bfB$, its
derivatives are not zero at the $X$ point.

Magnetic lines near the $X$ point are either hyperbolas or
intersecting separatrices passing through the point.  The separatrices
are the directions where the field is collinear with the displacement,
$B_z/B_x = \pm(z-Z)/(x-X)$.  The desired direction is
$T=(z-Z)/(x-X)=\tan(\theta_0)$, where $\theta_0=36^\circ$ is the
required angle between the field and the board plane ($54^\circ$ with
respect to the normal).  The collinearity condition yields
\begin{equation}
\frac{B_{z,x} + B_{z,z}T}{B_{x,x}+B_{x,z}T} = \pm T.
\end{equation}
For curl-free field, we have $B_{z,x} = B_{x,z}=0$, so the angle
condition involves only 3 derivatives.  For the plus sign above, the
slope of the separatrix is determined from the quadratic equation
\begin{equation}
B_{x,z}T^2 + (B_{x,x} - B_{z,z})T - B_{z,x} = 0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
T=\frac{C+\sqrt{C^2+4B_{x,z}B_{z,x}}}{2B_{x,z}},\quad C\equiv B_{z,z}-B_{x,x}.
\end{equation}
This expression agrees with the fact [known to AI] that the separatrix
lines are the eigenvectors of the feld's Jacobian matrix:
\begin{equation}
J=\left(\begin{array}{ll} B_{x,x} & B_{x,z}\\ B_{z,x} & B_{z,z}\end{array}\right),\quad J\bfv=\lambda\bfv,\quad
\bfv_{1,2}=\left(C\pm\sqrt{C^2+4B_{x,z}B_{z,x}}, 2B_{x,z}\right).
\label{v12}
\end{equation}
Note that the two eignevectors in (\ref{v12}) are orthogonal:
$\bfv_1\cdot\bfv_2=0$ due to the curl-free field, $B_{x,z}=B_{z,x}$,
as expected.  The expression for the eigenvectors in {\tt mark\_x()}
is used for a visual check of the $X$ point separatrix relative to
magnetic field lines.  If the $x$ axis is directed right and $z$ up,
then $v_{1x}>0$.  The orientation of the the vectors is as in the picture.

\section{Gradient}

Given that the direction $\bfv$ of the separatrix near the null point
is collinear with the direction of $\bfB$, the gradient of
$|\bfB|=(\bfv\cdot\bfB)/|\bfv|$ along $\bfv$ can be written as
\begin{equation}
|\bfna B| = |\bfv|^{-2}(\bfv\cdot\bfna)(\bfv\cdot\bfB).
\end{equation}
Using the linear expansion of the components of $\bfB$ in
(\ref{linear}), and the fact that $B_{x,z}=B_{z,x}$, the gradient is
\begin{equation}
  |\bfna B|=\frac{v_x^2B_{x,x} + 2v_xv_zB_{x,z}+v_z^2B_{z,z}}{v_x^2+v_z^2}.
  \label{grad}
\end{equation}

\section{Optimization of magnetic configuration}

We are concerned with 2D magnetic geometry in the $(x,z)$ plane.  If
the null point is to be located at $(x_0, z_0)$ at an angle
$\theta_0$, the current loop configuration can be found iteratively by
minimizing the utilty
\begin{equation}
  U = \bfB(x_0,0,z_0)|^2 + (\theta-\theta_0)^2(\theta-\theta_1)^2.
\end{equation}
This is assuming the field and the angles are numerically of the same
order of magnitude.
Since the beam can be shot in either first or second quadrant, both
$\theta_0=36^\circ$ and $\theta_1=54^\circ$ would work.

Since Biot-Savart simulation generates $B_x(x,z)$, $B_z(x,z)$ and
their derivatives, the angle $\theta$ is best expressed in terms of
the separatrix vectors $\bfv$ in (\ref{v12}).  If $T=\tan(\theta_0)$,
for either vector $\bfv_1$ or $\bfv_2$ we need to have
\begin{equation}
  |v_z/v_x| = T\quad\text{or}\quad1/T.
\end{equation}
This can be written as a zero angle penalty without guessing the
quadrant of the angle:
\begin{equation}
  U_\theta=\left[\log^2\left|\frac{v_z}{v_x}\right| - \log^2(T)\right]^2 = 0
\end{equation}

\subsection{Gradient optimization}

Gradient (\ref{grad}) depends on the choice of the vector $\bfv$.  The
choice must be made for the vector having the angle
$\pm\theta_0$ or $\pm\theta_0+180^\circ$ WRT the $x$ axis.
Thus we need to choose $\bfv_i$ such that
\begin{equation}
  \left|\frac{v_z}{v_x}\right| = T,
\end{equation}
or just the one with a smaller $|{v_z}/v_x|$ ratio.  Denote this
``relevant'' separatrix vector by $\bfv$ -- without an index.  Then we
can formulate the new utility
\begin{equation}
  \begin{aligned}
    U   &= U_B + \tau U_\theta + \gamma U_{grad},\\
    U_B &= |\bfB(x_0,0,z_0)|^2,\\
    U_\theta &= \left(\left|\frac{v_z}{v_x}\right| - T\right)^2,\\
    U_{grad} &= -\left|\frac{v_x^2B_{x,x} + 2v_xv_zB_{x,z}+v_z^2B_{z,z}}{v_x^2+v_z^2}\right|^2.
  \end{aligned}
  \label{U3}
\end{equation}
where $\bfv$ is the relevant one of the two in Eq.~(\ref{v12}).

For zero $\gamma$, (\ref{U3}) is minimized by by null location and
angle only reaching a small value of order $10^{-10}$ determined by
the accuracy of numerical Biot-Savart integration.  For all sizes of
order 1 cm and $n\gg1$ current loops, $B\sim n$, so $\tau$ needs to be
of order $n$ or larger for better control of the angle.

The third (gradient) term has the same structure is the first term
which we want to be very close to zero, so we must use $\gamma\ll1$.
Adding a small $\gamma$ factor will drive $U$ negative and introduce a
trade-off between exact geometry and good gradient.  The optimal value
of $\gamma$ is not {\em a priori} known and needs to be experimented
with.  As an option, the first two (geometry) terms in (\ref{U3}) can
use absolute values instead of squares.  That would make a stronger
stimiulus to have the right null point at a right angle.

A problem with the gradient term is that the optimizer can happily
increase the gradient at the expense of minimizing $U_B$ and
$U_\theta$.  It would be nice to have an algorithm maximizing
$U_{grad}$ subject to $U_B=U_\theta=0$ as constraints, but this type
of optimizer appears difficult to implement.  Still there is
\href{https://docs.scipy.org/doc/scipy/reference/generated/scipy.optimize.NonlinearConstraint.html}{\tt
    scipy.ooptimize.NonlinearConstraint}.

\section{Geometry constraints}

$i$th circle in a ``spiral'' has the $x$ position and center and the
radius
\begin{equation}
  x_i = c + i\,\delta x,\quad r_i=a + i\,\delta r,\quad i<n,
\end{equation}
where $\delta x$ is ``excentricity,'' or center displacement per
``revolution,'' and $\delta r>0$ is the radius increment.  To avoid
self-intersection of the circles, we must obviously have $|\delta
x|<\delta r$.  The coil densities on the two sides are $(\delta r \pm
\delta x)^{-1}$.  The max coil density $\rho=25\,cm^{-1}$ should
be realized on one side, meaning that the radius increment is either
fixed for concentric circles or uniquely determined by the
excentricity:
\begin{equation}
  \delta r = \rho^{-1} + |\delta x|
\end{equation}
The outermost circle extends to
\begin{equation}
  x_{\pm} = x_{n-1} \pm r_{n-1}.
  \label{xpm}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Fitting maximum number of coils}
}
To maximize the field, and possibly its gradient, we can require the
maximum number of coils $n$ such as one of the limits (\ref{xpm})
reaches the board boundaries $\pm X$ where $X=5\,cm$.  At least such a
requirement would allow to minimize $X$.  So we want the first of the
following two equations to hold as $n$ is increased:
\begin{equation}
  c + (n - 1)\delta x \pm [a + (n - 1)(\rho^{-1} + |\delta x|)] = \pm X.
\end{equation}
This gives
\begin{equation}
  \begin{aligned}
    n - 1
    &=  \min\left(\frac{\pm (X - a) - c}{\delta x \pm (\rho^{-1} + |\delta x|)}\right)\\
    &= \min\left(
    \frac{X - a - c}{\rho^{-1}+2\delta x^+},
    \frac{-X + a - c}{-\rho^{-1}+2\delta x^-}\right)\\
    &= \min\left(
    \frac{X - a - c}{\rho^{-1}+2\delta x^+},
    \frac{X - a + c}{\rho^{-1}-2\delta x^-}\right).
  \end{aligned}
\end{equation}
Consistency conditin $n>1$ requires $a<X$ and $|c|<X-a$. Since {\tt
  scipy.minimize} supports only rectangular constrains, {SpiralBounds}
have to be more restrictive.  As an option, the utility function can
return a large (bad) number for an invalid spiral geometry.

With these constraints, there are just 3 free parameters of a
``spiral:'' the inner center and radius and the excentricity $(c, a,
\delta x)$.  If desired, the half-size of the board $X$ can also be
played with.  For two spirals and the current ratio {\tt Ir}, we have
a 7D search space ({\tt c1,c2,a1,a2,dx1,dx2,Ir}).  For multiple loop
layers to support higher total current and field, the search space
will be larger unless some layers are synchronized, that is, made
identical except height $z$.

\subsection{Geometry targeting the right null point}

Fitting in hundreds of coils in a pan-like disk may be not the best
geometry as we must have a sufficient curvature of magnetic lines to
make the twi fields cancel at the $X$ point.  A protype is just two
circular loops.
